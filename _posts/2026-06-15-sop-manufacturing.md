In most manufacturing businesses I've worked with, the financial risk isn't fraud — it's drift. Floor stock slowly stops matching the ledger, small discrepancies get waved off as "normal," and by year-end nobody can say with confidence why the numbers don't reconcile.

A good Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) doesn't need to be complicated. It needs to be specific, followed consistently, and checked against actual physical counts on a regular schedule. Here's how I approach it.

## 1. Start with physical-to-ledger counts

The single most useful control in a manufacturing environment is a routine physical stock count reconciled against the books — not just at year-end for the auditors, but monthly or even weekly for high-value or fast-moving items. Discrepancies caught early are cheap to fix. Discrepancies caught in December are expensive, and sometimes unexplainable.

## 2. Write the SOP for the person doing the job, not for the auditor

A lot of SOPs are written to satisfy an audit checklist, and end up unused on a shelf. A working SOP reads like a checklist someone can actually follow on the factory floor — clear steps, clear sign-offs, and a clear escalation path when something doesn't match.

## 3. Track variances as a trend, not a one-off

A single stock variance might be a counting error. The same variance showing up three months in a row is a process problem — maybe a supplier is short-shipping, maybe there's wastage nobody's accounting for. Logging variances over time, rather than resolving each one in isolation, is what actually surfaces the root cause.

## 4. Keep it audit-ready by default

If your SOPs are followed consistently through the year, audit season stops being a scramble. The records already exist — you're just handing them over. That's the real payoff of good internal controls: not passing an audit, but never having to prepare for one.
