Audit & assurance

Substantive testing checklist

Support notes for accountants who prepare schedules for auditors or perform internal substantive checks.

Assertions — keep them on the working paper

Assertion (simplified)Question you are answering
Existence / occurrenceDid this balance or transaction really happen?
CompletenessIs anything missing that should be recorded?
Accuracy / valuationAre amounts correct and valued properly?
Rights & obligationsDoes the entity own it / owe it?
Cut-offIs it in the right period?
PresentationIs classification and disclosure fair?

High-yield substantive ideas by area

  • Bank: reconcile to statement; test reconciling items to clearing.
  • Receivables: after-date cash, confirmations where used, allowance rationale.
  • Payables: supplier statements, after-date invoices for completeness.
  • Inventory: count attendance results, NRV tests on aged lines.
  • PPE: additions to invoice, disposals, depreciation recalculation.
  • Revenue: sequence tests, cut-off around period end, unusual journals.

Working paper discipline

  • Source, date, preparer, reviewer initials.
  • Sample method stated (not only “selected items”).
  • Exceptions listed with conclusion (adjusted / accepted / escalated).

Worked example: substantive testing on trade receivables

Say year-end trade receivables total Rs. 8,400,000 across 140 customer accounts. A common approach: stratify by value, test 100% of balances above a set threshold (say Rs. 200,000, covering perhaps 60% of the balance by value in a typical customer mix), and sample the remainder using monetary unit sampling.

  • Existence: send confirmations to the high-value tier; for non-responses, fall back to after-date cash receipts as an alternative procedure — don't just drop the item from testing.
  • Valuation: for the allowance for receivables, recalculate the aged analysis and challenge management's assumptions on any account over 90 days that still shows no allowance.
  • Cut-off: trace the last five sales invoices before year-end and the first five after — check they're recorded in the correct period against dispatch/delivery evidence, not just invoice date.

The write-up matters as much as the test: state the population, the sampling method, the threshold used, and — critically — what you did about exceptions. "No exceptions noted" only means something if the reviewer can see exactly what was tested.

Where this sits in the ACCA syllabus

Substantive procedures are central to Audit and Assurance (AA), and the same assertions framework carries into Advanced Audit and Assurance (AAA), where the emphasis shifts from "can you list the tests" to "can you justify why this test responds to this specific assessed risk" — examiners consistently penalise generic, boilerplate procedures that aren't tied to the scenario.

Common pitfalls

  • Testing the wrong assertion — a confirmation letter primarily addresses existence, not valuation; pairing it with the wrong risk in your working paper is a common exam and practice error.
  • "Selected items" as a sampling method — reviewers (and examiners) want to see the actual basis: haphazard, systematic interval, monetary unit, or judgmental with stated criteria.
  • Skipping the "so what" — an exception found but not followed through to a conclusion (adjusted, accepted as immaterial, or escalated) leaves the file incomplete.
  • Over-relying on one procedure — for higher-risk balances, corroborate a confirmation with a second, independent procedure rather than treating one clean response as sufficient evidence on its own.

Designing a substantive procedure

A substantive procedure should respond to an identified risk of material misstatement at assertion level. Start with the balance or transaction class, identify the relevant assertion, understand what could be misstated, and choose a procedure that produces evidence about that risk. A generic instruction such as “check invoices” is not enough unless it states what is being checked and why.

Direction of testing matters

  • To test existence of recorded assets, select items from the accounting records and trace them to physical evidence or independent documentation.
  • To test completeness of liabilities, select evidence such as post-year-end invoices or supplier statements and trace it back to the ledger or accruals listing.
  • To test occurrence of revenue, select recorded sales and inspect orders, dispatch evidence and customer documentation.
  • To test cut-off, inspect transactions immediately before and after the reporting date and assess the period in which they were recorded.

Examples by financial statement area

AreaRisk / assertionIllustrative procedure
Cash at bankExistence and accuracyObtain bank confirmation where appropriate; agree balances to bank statements and independently reperform reconciliations.
Trade receivablesExistence and valuationSend confirmations where suitable; inspect subsequent receipts; evaluate aged and disputed balances and the allowance estimate.
Trade payablesCompletenessInspect post-year-end payments, supplier statements and unmatched invoices for liabilities omitted at year-end.
InventoryExistence and valuationAttend counts where required, perform test counts, inspect condition and compare cost with NRV evidence.
Property, plant and equipmentExistence, rights and valuationInspect material additions, invoices and title evidence; recalculate depreciation and examine disposal proceeds.
RevenueOccurrence and cut-offTrace selected entries to customer orders and dispatch evidence; inspect documents around year-end.

Sampling and selection

Document the population, period, sampling unit, selection method, sample size rationale and treatment of exceptions. High-value or unusual items may be tested separately from a representative sample. A sample chosen because documents were easy to obtain may introduce bias. The appropriate sample depends on assessed risk, expected error, tolerable misstatement, population characteristics and the assurance required.

What makes evidence persuasive?

Evidence obtained directly from an independent external source is generally more reliable than internally generated evidence, although reliability depends on the circumstances. Original records, system-generated reports and management explanations should be evaluated for authenticity, completeness and consistency. Where information produced by the entity is used, test its accuracy and completeness as appropriate.

Recording exceptions and conclusions

  1. Describe the exception precisely and identify the source document and sample reference.
  2. Determine whether it is an isolated error, a systematic issue or evidence of a control failure.
  3. Quantify the known and projected misstatement where relevant.
  4. Discuss the matter with the appropriate person and obtain corroborating evidence; an explanation alone may not resolve it.
  5. Conclude whether additional procedures, an adjustment or escalation is required.

Audit reminder: This guide is educational support, not a complete audit programme. The auditor remains responsible for applying the relevant auditing standards, assessing risks, determining materiality and designing procedures for the specific engagement.
