IFRS 16 Leases: A Practical Guide

June 02, 2026 · 5 min read

Before IFRS 16, most operating leases stayed off the balance sheet entirely — you paid rent, expensed it, and moved on. That changed. Under IFRS 16 (and the equivalent NFRS 16 here in Nepal), almost every lease now shows up on the balance sheet as a Right-of-Use (ROU) asset, matched by a lease liability.

The concept is straightforward. The practical application is where most teams run into trouble. Here's what actually trips people up.

1. Choosing the discount rate

The lease liability is measured at the present value of future lease payments, discounted using the rate implicit in the lease — or, if that's not readily determinable (which is most of the time), the lessee's incremental borrowing rate. Getting this rate wrong throws off both the liability and the ROU asset from day one, and the error compounds every reporting period after.

Practical Example

A 5-year office lease at Rs. 100,000/month, discounted at a 10% incremental borrowing rate, is recognized as a lease liability at the present value of those 60 payments — not the simple total of Rs. 6,000,000. The difference between the two is significant, and it's the first thing an auditor checks.

2. Tracking lease modifications and renewal options

Leases rarely stay static. Renewal options, early termination clauses, and rent renegotiations all require you to reassess the liability and the ROU asset. The most common gap I see in practice is that these modifications happen operationally — someone renegotiates rent with a landlord — without the change ever reaching the accounting team, so the books stay stale.

3. Separating lease and non-lease components

A lease for a warehouse might bundle in maintenance or security services. Under IFRS 16, non-lease components should be separated out and expensed normally, rather than capitalized into the ROU asset — unless you elect the practical expedient to combine them. Missing this distinction inflates the asset and liability beyond what they should be.

4. Short-term and low-value exemptions

Leases of 12 months or less, and leases of low-value assets (like laptops or small office equipment), can be kept off the balance sheet and expensed on a straight-line basis instead. This exemption is useful, but easy to apply inconsistently if there isn't a clear internal policy on what counts as "low-value" for your organization.

None of this is conceptually difficult. It just requires a system for capturing lease changes as they happen, rather than reconstructing them from memory at year-end.
