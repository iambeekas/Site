Bridging Tax Theory and Practice

May 24, 2026 · 4 min read

Teaching Advanced Taxation, the hardest thing to get across isn't the rules themselves — students can memorize provisions. It's helping them see how those provisions actually play out when you're sitting across from a client's ledger, not a textbook problem.

A few things I keep coming back to in lectures, because they matter more in practice than the syllabus suggests.

1. Exam questions are clean. Real clients aren't.

A textbook problem gives you every fact you need, cleanly labeled. A real client gives you a shoebox of receipts, half-remembered transactions, and "I think that was for the business, but I'm not sure." Part of what I try to teach is how to ask the right questions to reconstruct a clean picture from a messy one — because that's most of the actual job.

2. Substantive testing is a mindset, not a checklist

Students often treat audit testing as a list of procedures to tick off. In practice, good substantive testing starts with a question: "if something were wrong here, what would it look like?" — and then designs a test to catch exactly that. The checklist should follow the thinking, not replace it.

In the Classroom

I like walking through real (anonymized) scenarios where the "obvious" answer from theory doesn't hold up once you factor in how a business actually operates — it's usually the fastest way to make a rule stick.

3. Tax law changes; judgment doesn't

Provisions get amended, thresholds shift, new schedules appear. What stays constant is the underlying judgment — understanding intent behind a rule well enough to apply it to a situation the rule didn't explicitly anticipate. That's the skill I try to build, because it's the one that survives the next amendment.

If there's one thing I want students to leave with, it's this: passing the exam proves you understand the rules. Practice is where you learn to work with the mess the rules were written for.
